Reviewer peek

Drag that button to your bookmarks bar. Clicking it here does nothing useful — it has to run on the page you are reading.

Using it

  1. Select a name. A CMS reviewer row, a line in a spreadsheet, a name in an e-mail — all work. Dragging across the whole row is fine; the id and the affiliation are stripped.
  2. Click the bookmark. A panel opens at the top right.
  3. Read, then close it. You can also type a different name into the box.

What you get

For each person who matches the name: h-index, number of works, total citations, affiliation history, and their main research topics. That is usually enough to tell a senior researcher from a first-year student in about two seconds, which is the question you are normally asking. “Recent papers” lists their last five, with year and venue, for when the counts alone do not settle it.

Several candidates are shown rather than one. Common names are the whole difficulty: searching Koichiro Yoshino returns a speech researcher, an oncologist and a chemist. A green bar marks a speech or audio record, and the affiliation separates the rest at a glance. A tool that picked one for you would pick wrong often enough to be dangerous.

Where the data comes from

OpenAlex, which is open, needs no account and allows direct queries from the page. Google Scholar has no API and blocks automated access, so no tool can read it — the panel links out to Scholar, DBLP and Semantic Scholar for when you want to look yourself.

What it does not do

It keeps nothing. No list of who you looked up, no cache, no cookie, no server of ours in the path. The only request is the OpenAlex query, and it carries an e-mail address purely because OpenAlex asks callers to identify themselves for rate limiting.
